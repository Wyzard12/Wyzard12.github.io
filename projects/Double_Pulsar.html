Solo Game Designer & Development

CODENAME DOULBE PULSAR

IN SHORT

Developed as a competitive intake assignment for Breda University of Applied Sciences (BUas), Double Pulsar is a 2D side-scrolling platformer designed to demonstrate my core competency in analytical game design, rapid prototyping, and systemic intent. Facing severe asset constraints and unexpected technical physics hurdles, I executed a critical, mid-project design pivot—rebuilding the game from scratch to safeguard player experience against motion sickness and scope creep. This portfolio piece details how I prioritized a highly polished, functional design framework over flawed complexity, transforming a technical failure into a masterclass in risk management and player-centric onboarding.

To try out the game by yourself, please navigate to the Codename Double Pulsar itch.io page.

PROJECT OVERVIEW & CORE CONSTRAINTS

The primary objective of the BUas intake assessment was not merely to deliver a finished game, but to showcase a transparent, rigorous game design methodology. The evaluation focused heavily on the "why" behind every design decision: justifying mechanical choices, defining a clear game loop, ensuring intuitive progression visibility, and establishing an empirical feedback loop through external playtesting.

Relying on my four years of foundational experience, I selected Unity as my development environment. The brief demanded a 1-to-3 level platformer bound by a rigid structural constraint: no external custom art assets were allowed, restricting production strictly to self-made assets or the open-source Kenney library. Lacking professional art skills, I turned this limitation into a design driver. By analyzing the simple, static animation frames available in the Kenney library, I reverse-engineered my movement parameters. Instead of coding arbitrary physics and forcing art to fit, I tuned the acceleration, deceleration, and jump arcs to match the visual limits of the assets, ensuring the player avatar felt like a highly reactive entity rather than an ungrounded geometric primitive.

Choosing a 2D format allowed me to allocate more cognitive bandwidth to high-level system concepting and documentation. However, this introduced a steep learning curve; my academic background was predominantly 3D-focused, and I had zero formal experience in 2D level design. To avoid creating arbitrary, unreadable pacing layouts, I deconstructed the environmental mechanics of Blasphemous—specifically studying how it subtly teaches players mechanics through level geometry. I then cross-referenced these case studies with collaborative peer workshops to draft my initial structural pacing models.

PHASE 1: CONCEPTUALIZATION & SYSTEMIC INTENT

Because the restricted art assets lacked complex combat animations, the gameplay depth had to be derived entirely from systemic complexity rather than visceral action. I wanted to push past basic shapes like cubes and circles to challenge myself to create an immersive, responsive feel even within tight limitations. The chosen core mechanical hook became Gravity Manipulation, where players navigate volatile environments by exploiting low-gravity pockets to solve structural traversal puzzles.

To anchor this mechanic, the narrative provided immediate player alignment: an astronaut stranded in deep space must navigate perilous cosmic sectors to restore communication with their crew. Inspired by the wind-propulsion mechanics in the "Where Olive Trees Wither" zone of Blasphemous, this simple narrative setup answered the critical design question of player motivation ("Why must I cross this chasm?").

The initial production roadmap mapped out a clean Tutorial ➔ Mastery pacing curve distributed across three macro-environments:

➔ Level 1 (Space Exterior): Introducing core movement parameters, camera tracking, and basic low-gravity physics, culminating in unlocking a Jetpack gadget.
➔ Level 2 (Asteroid Cavern): Escalating spatial difficulty through hazardous environmental geometry, requiring players to master a Double Jump upgrade.
➔ Level 3 (The Core): A systematic gauntlet requiring players to analyze randomized hazard patterns, forcing them to combine all previously mastered upgrades to survive.

TECHNICAL FAILURE & THE CRITICAL PIVOT

Once active production began on the low-gravity space prototype, the project suffered critical mechanical complications. My lack of advanced space-physics equations and rotational calculus resulted in a highly erratic camera system and clumsy momentum controls. A severe systemic bug continually zeroed out the player's velocity when transitioning between independent asteroid gravity fields, stripping the gameplay of natural, fluid movement.

More catastrophically, the prototype broke a fundamental prompt constraint: it had ceased to be a true side-scroller. The dynamic rotating camera caused severe spatial disorientation and motion sickness—a critical failure in UX design. Recognizing that continuing down this path risked missing the hard deadline and failing the core project requirements, I detached my ego from the concept and made the executive decision to scrap the prototype entirely.

This "trial by fire" taught me a vital industry lesson: the distinct line between theoretical concept and practical execution. Risking a high-stakes project on unvetted, high-risk technical mechanics can jeopardize an entire production cycle.

Returning to a clean slate, I pivoted to a lower-risk, highly stable design: a traditional side-scrolling framework reminiscent of Super Mario Bros, but adapted to my mechanical style. I scaled down the scope but retained the core narrative and successful mechanics. Instead of deep-space survival, the astronaut now navigated an abandoned facility. To bridge my level design deficit under a tight deadline, I co-analyzed layout drafts with a peer, dissecting how 2D levels establish structural flows before reshaping the final layouts into my own unique design vision.

The initial conceptual sketches were transformed directly into a structural roadmap, serving as the definitive blueprint for the finalized level geometry.

ANALYSIS OF ONBOARDING & UI HIERARCHY

Recognizing that a player’s initial interaction dictates their long-term engagement, I heavily prioritized a low-friction onboarding experience for Level 1. The level layout directly mirrors the initial conceptual sketch, transitioning from a paper blueprint into a fully functional introductory environment. Because onboarding requires safe spaces to practice mechanics, I opted for a "forgiving design" philosophy: environmental hazards are minimized, and failing a platforming challenge never issues fatal punishment. Instead, the level design routes players into organic failure loops where they can safely retry and master the mechanics.

This analytical approach to early pacing was validated during the playtesting phase, where 100% of playtesters successfully navigated the introductory stage without frustration or technical confusion.

Visual Tutorialization & UI Hierarchy

To complement this spatial onboarding, I adopted a strict "show, don't tell" framework for the UI design. I bypassed heavy text descriptions for fundamental movement mechanics—such as walking, jumping, and dashing—and instead integrated minimalist, universal vector icons directly into the world environment. This structural choice reduced cognitive overload, allowing players to immediately absorb contextual controls without halting their gameplay momentum.

For advanced, system-specific mechanics that required deeper explanation, I implemented contextual tutorial panels. These UI menus were stylistically inspired by the dark, immersive presentation windows found in Blasphemous, balancing informative clarity with the game’s atmospheric facility aesthetic to keep the player fully immersed even during onboarding moments.

HAZARD INTEGRATION & SYSTEMIC DIFFICULTY SCALING

To prevent the standardized side-scrolling loop from becoming flat or monotonous, I introduced structural environmental hazards and static enemies to inject mechanical tension and layer tactical depth. These elements included classic platforming hurdles such as moving saw blades, automated projectiles/turrets, and hazardous toxic waste zones.

Rather than deploying these traps arbitrarily, I utilized a compound design methodology. Hazards were introduced individually in safe contexts for player acclimation, then layered dynamically to create complex spatial puzzles.

This architecture culminated in Level 3—the game's design climax—where all hazardous elements converged systematically. This forced players to synchronize their movement mechanics, requiring high-velocity reflexes and precise timing to navigate structural gauntlets without triggering a punishing "domino effect" of consecutive damage. The final layout offered an optimized balance in difficulty tuning—delivering an experience that is accessible to newcomers yet engaging for experienced platformer players.

PLAYTESTING, AUDIENCE DIVERSITY & ITERATION

A game cannot be considered functionally complete until it has survived external playtesting. Developing in a vacuum creates a developer blind spot, raising the probability of encountering critical bugs and disjointed user experiences. To satisfy the strict requirements of the BUas intake assignment and eliminate bias, I conducted a structured playtest pipeline utilizing a diverse demographic of seven external participants, completely disconnected from the project's development.

To test the accessibility of my onboarding systems, I selected a wide target demographic ranging from young adults (ages 18–20) to older users (over age 60). This broad spectrum allowed me to measure control legibility and mechanical comprehension across varying degrees of gaming literacy.

The empirical data collected from these sessions was instrumental in refining the final build:

➔ The Early Phase (Friction Points): The first two playtesters encountered game-breaking bugs and physics inconsistencies that significantly disrupted their experience. I treated these failures as critical telemetry, halting the tests to implement immediate technical hotfixes.
➔ The Late Phase (Validation): Following these adjustments, the remaining five testers reported high satisfaction, navigating the levels fluidly and confirming that the progression curve felt natural yet engaging.

CONCLUSION & PERSONAL REFLECTION

This documentation archives the complete evolutionary arc of Double Pulsar, showcasing the reality of independent game production. By mapping out initial conceptual flaws, documenting critical technical failures, and detailing the systemic pivots that secured the final product, this project serves as concrete evidence of my readiness for advanced game design study.

It explicitly demonstrates my capacity for objective self-critique, my understanding of player psychology, and my willingness to confidently scrap a flawed prototype to protect the ultimate user experience. This journey has firmly established my foundational belief that great game design is not a product of first impulses, but a structured framework forged through relentless, disciplined iteration.

01 / Ego Detachment & Scope Control

Demonstrated the professional maturity required to kill a flawed prototype early. By prioritizing user health (mitigating motion sickness) and deadline security over personal investment, I successfully insulated the project from fatal scope creep.

02 / Constraint-Driven Reverse Engineering

Transformed a severe art asset deficit into a mechanical strength. Designing character movement formulas around the literal frame-counts of the available Kenney assets ensured a tight, responsive gameplay loop.

03 / Data-Driven Polish & Iteration

Established a structured, multi-generational playtesting model that stripped away developer bias, translating raw user friction and early build telemetry into objective, balance-improving hotfixes.
